Atomic mass is trickier than atomic number, which is a simple whole number because it just counts protons. But atomic mass is almost always a messy decimal. To see why, let's look at a lump of pure carbon. You might think every atom here is identical, but atoms of the same element can have different weights. These are called isotopes. They have the exact same protons, but a different number of neutrons. Carbon 12 has of neutrons. Carbon 12 has six neutrons, while carbon 14 has eight. Because their weights vary, a single whole number won't work. Instead, the periodic table shows a weighted average. The math averages all naturally occurring isotopes together, tipping heavily toward carbon 12 because it's vastly more abundant. That average is exactly why atomic mass is usually a decimal, and that heavier carbon-14, its extra neutrons make it radioactive, which help scientists date ancient fossils.
